The foreground is vital in landscape. It not only balances the composition, but also adds texture, lens tension, and light modeling. It can also simplify the frame and make the subject stand out. You could say there is no landscape without a foreground. So how should you choose a foreground in autumn to make your work more attractive? [JF:Page]

Autumn shooting calls for elements that show the season. So what makes the best foreground in autumn?

1. Leaves

Leaves are the most-used autumn foreground. Scatter a handful of fallen leaves in front of the lens. This highlights the autumn mood and the lens tension.

You may ask why others scatter leaves so evenly. To shoot this, fix the camera on a tripod, toss leaves several times, take many shots, then stack them in post, or combine them with masks.

You can also use a tilt-shift lens for a unique mood.

3. See below for "How one leaf can capture the feeling of the whole autumn." If you are new, mastering one leaf beats trying to capture the whole autumn.

Shoot upward with leaves as foreground.

Use a wide lens to exaggerate the foreground. This is probably the most popular selfie technique.

A wide lens suits fallen leaves, moss, stones, and flowing water. Only a wide lens makes the leaves stand out.

2. Branches

A foreground can simplify the frame. If the background is a lake, blue sky, distant mountains, or a river, first consider shooting at the wide end.

Then you can use the telephoto end to show just one part.

Dry branches and dead wood are the best foreground. To turn water into mist, expose for 30 seconds or more. By day you need an ND1000 filter, and at dusk an ND8 or a CPL (a CPL usually gives three stops of light reduction).

Learn the difference between a lens and the human eye, and learn to analyze the subject through the lens. For example, messy dry branches look especially picturesque through a wide lens.

3. Wild grass

Wild grass and stones are common, so they are the easiest foreground to overlook. Large patches of wild grass need a wide lens.

A single stalk of wild grass suits a telephoto shot against backlight.

The size contrast between wild grass and the woods is clear in front of the lens. A few stalks or a large patch of grass can both serve as foreground. When you have little else to use, common wild grass comes in handy.

When the foreground really runs out, you can use a panoramic stitch to show a wide view.

4. Finding lines

Orderly lines, such as a path, a tree trunk, or a chair, gain rhythm through a wide lens and catch the eye.

Shoot one tree and move close to the trunk to show its texture. To keep the whole frame sharp, focus is the key. Usually place the focus at one third of the frame, or set the focus ring to infinity and then back it off a little to maximize depth of field.

A high angle with a wide lens can make the frame messy. A low angle both highlights the foreground and shows the wide lens sense of extension.

5. Foreground bokeh

To highlight foreground bokeh, first choose a good foreground, then use a telephoto and a wide-aperture lens well.

 1. Use a telephoto to compress the water and reflect bokeh. Dense bokeh draws the viewer in.

2. Use a wide-aperture lens to blur the foreground. Note: do not shoot toward the sun with the aperture wide open.

3. Mask the lens with a transparent or semi-transparent object that refracts light, and you can capture an interesting frame.

6. Backlit silhouettes

Such a frame is beautiful because it blends the near, middle, and far ground into one. The strong foreground lines, together with the person clothing and posture, show the charm of the lens and the story of the person.

Whether you use a wide or a telephoto lens, the key to silhouettes is choosing a distinctive foreground that sets off the subject.

There are many other things that can serve as foreground, beyond the subjects here. It is best to find something fresh and unusual as your foreground. The point of this article is simple: when you do not know how to shoot, or the frame looks ordinary, use foreground composition more often.
