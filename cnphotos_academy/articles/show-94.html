Is an Ultra-Wide Lens Hard to Use? Learn These 11 Tips for Great Autumn Photos!

Published: 2022-09-28

Source link: https://www.cnphotos.net/academy/show-94.html

Photography and lens performance go hand in hand. Because ultra-wide lenses have strong resolution, most photographers like them, but few use them well. This article gives you advice on how to use an ultra-wide lens.

Why outdoor landscape shots often use a wide angle

For example, shooting a river

1. Always use a small aperture

Almost no lens gives its best image at the widest aperture, so stop down when you shoot. In my experience, F8 to F16 works well for landscapes. Note that too small an aperture causes diffraction and makes the photo less sharp. As a rule, F16 is small enough, and F8 to F11 is best.

Aperture also affects depth of field. A wide aperture blurs some areas and keeps others sharp. With a small aperture, the photo is fairly clear from near to far.

2. Use the lowest ISO

Most cameras go down to ISO 100, and some like the D810 go to 64. Shooting at the lowest ISO gives a clean image with no visible noise. For landscapes, we usually use the lowest ISO.

I do not recommend the expanded ISO (most cameras can extend to 50). In my experience, ISO 50 does not look good.

The exception is the Milky Way. At night the light is poor, so you may need ISO 1600 or 3200. But the rule still holds: the lower the better. I have shot the Milky Way at ISO 200 and 400, and it still works. Choose your ISO to fit the scene.

3. Use a tripod

With a small aperture and low ISO, the shutter slows down. Handheld shooting may blur the frame. So use a tripod. A steady tripod makes your photos sharper. For Chinese tripods, I recommend Benro and SIRUI. For a one-time buy, go with Gitzo.

4. Use the level

Most cameras have a level, and many tripods have one too. Why level the camera? When you shoot a horizon, a tilted frame feels wrong. In most cases, a level horizon just looks right.

5. Focus correctly

A small aperture gives deeper depth of field, but focus still matters. For landscapes, I recommend live view, zoom in 10x, and focus by hand. It is the safest way.

Where do you focus? Some know the hyperfocal distance, but it is complex. In my experience, focus on an object that is neither near nor far, or about one third into the frame. It is a simple method that works most of the time.

6. Keep buildings vertical

I have seen many photos where buildings lean inward when shot with an ultra-wide. That is the perspective distortion of the ultra-wide. Most of the time, keep buildings vertical so they match how we see the real world.

What if you shoot it crooked? No problem. Fix it later in Lightroom.

Are there good photos with leaning buildings? Yes. But they are usually close-ups, or they lean on purpose to give the viewer a sense of pressure.

7. Shoot from below, try portrait

The wide view makes an object feel taller and grander in portrait. Remember: get lower, and then lower again.

8. Shoot from above

If you work hard, or get lucky, and reach a summit or a cliff edge, try shooting down. Looking at the ground from high up feels great, and the shots look grand.

9. Use a foreground to lift the photo

An ultra-wide takes in more of the scene and a wider view, but the photo can lack a subject. That is why you need a foreground. A good foreground makes the photo stronger.

10. Include the sun in the frame

The wide view can even include the sun in your photo. Such shots are rare, so try it. Making an unusual photo is the joy of photography.

Remember, a bright sun can leave the ground very dark. Then you need post-processing or another method to fix it.

11. Use filters

Filters are a big topic, so I will not cover them all. I recommend a round ND filter, also called a neutral-density filter. Put it on the lens and slow the shutter. It suits scenes with water or clouds. It makes the clouds feel alive and the water look silky.

For clouds and flowing water, an ND500 or ND1000 is enough.

Using a wide angle

Shoot very close to the foreground: With the foreground in focus, move as close as you can to it. This brings out the full force of the wide angle.

Closed composition: Fill the frame. A closed composition feels visually pressing and pushes the power of the wide angle, so the result feels fresh.

A unique form: The form and texture of the foreground show the quality of the light, and they blend well after sharpening in post. So choose the foreground carefully, and the form must be unique.

Problems to avoid with a wide angle

With a wide angle, a key issue is that near objects look very large and far objects look very small. Some people use tilt-shift, panoramic stitching, or focus stacking to fix it.

When you shoot very close to a foreground, even the smallest aperture cannot keep the far scene sharp, so you need depth-of-field stacking.

How to choose a foreground
