Why Do Your Photos Lack Impact? Where Is the Problem?

Published: 2026-02-11

Focus is one of the most important steps in photography. Once an image is out of focus, there is no real fix for it. Whenever a photo misses focus, I delete it from the hard drive at the first stage of my editing workflow. Sharpening tools in image editing software keep getting better, but as far as I know, no tool can truly repair a photo that is out of focus.

Make sure the image is sharp at the spot you want. This must happen before you press the shutter, so the most important element in the photo has a sharp focus. Choosing the right focus area in the frame is critical. A small miss often ruins the photo outright. We must lock onto the focus point so we can highlight the area we want the viewer to look at. A clear, definite focus point makes any composition more impactful. People spend more time looking at a well-focused photo than at a blurry one.

In photo terms, focus also means the exact point where light converges inside the lens. When light enters the lens, the shape of the glass bends it and brings it to one point. To be clear, this article is about the area you should focus on in the composition.


What is the focal point of a photo?

The focal point of any photo is the spot where you most want people to look. It is the most engaging part of the photo, and usually where the lens focused when you shot it. The focal point is often the strongest part of the frame, but not always. In any composition, a carefully chosen focus is one of the key elements. Whatever the subject, a clear focus point makes the photo more engaging.

Choosing the focus position when you frame the shot is critical. Where the lens focuses affects how the viewer reads the photo. I notice that new photographers often place the focus in the center and center the subject. As they try different compositions, their choice of focus becomes more flexible. Once they know the basics, the focus no longer has to stay in the center. It can land at the end of a leading line, or at an intersection of the rule-of-thirds grid, or be set inside a frame-within-a-frame.


Choose your focal point

Usually the focal point is obvious when you compose, but sometimes it is not. When the subject has eyes, whether a person, an animal, a bird, or even a statue, I follow one rule: choose the eyes as the focal point. As long as the subject has eyes, I focus on them. If the eyes are soft, the photo never feels right.

But what if the frame holds several people, animals, or birds? Where do you focus then? You must pick one pair of eyes or one single eye as the focal point. It may be the eye closest to you, or it may not. Remember that your focus choice affects how others read the photo. In a busy frame, the focus point decides what the subject is.

I usually pick the subject for each photo before I raise the camera to my eye. I instinctively know where to focus, because that is why I press the shutter. When I find an interesting subject, I take one or two shots first, then try different compositions and focus points. Sometimes the most engaging focus is not obvious. Try focusing on different parts of the subject. This matters most when the viewfinder holds many elements.


Managing focus in shallow and deep depth-of-field composition

With shallow depth of field, precise focus is critical. No photo with the wrong focus point can be saved. Shallow depth of field allows no mistakes.

You must make sure the focus lands exactly in the right spot. This is especially true when the subject has eyes. If only one eye can be sharp, you must choose which one, usually the eye closest to the camera, but not always. The shallower the depth of field, the more careful you must be. When you use a wide aperture, a telephoto lens, and get close to the subject, accurate focus is especially hard. All of these factors affect depth of field and double the difficulty of focusing.

Using shallow depth of field is a great way to make the subject stand out. It separates the subject from the background and makes it pop in the frame. But if the focus point is wrong, the photo is not worth keeping or sharing. Try adjusting the aperture. If it is hard to keep the subject sharp, close the aperture a little. This keeps more of the subject sharp and improves the look.

When you shoot, try to keep the camera level with the subject. The more the subject is at the same distance from the camera, the larger the sharp area on the subject. If you can, move the subject away from the background. This keeps the background blur soft and smooth.


Getting the most from your camera's autofocus

Most of us prefer to use the camera's autofocus system. Modern cameras focus fast, and once they lock, they are very accurate. But you cannot always rely on the camera's autofocus. I usually use single-point autofocus. I like to place the focus point exactly where I want, so I can be sure the subject in the frame is sharp.

No matter how advanced your camera is, it cannot tell what your subject is. It usually picks the nearest thing to focus on, which may not be your subject. Or, if you use eye detection, it focuses on the eyes. That is why I keep control of the focus point myself. Handing the focus choice to the camera can cause problems, especially with shallow depth of field and no clear subject. As cameras improve, face recognition and eye detection make autofocus much easier. A camera that locks onto the eyes is great, but you still need to make sure it picks the eye you want. This is key when the frame holds several pairs of eyes.

If you are used to multi-point autofocus, try switching to single-point mode. It takes time to get used to placing the focus point by hand, but with practice you will get much faster. As you gain experience, you will place the focus point more quickly, and more of your photos will be sharp.

Choose your focus position carefully. A photo that is out of focus cannot be saved. Sharpening tools can help a slightly soft image, but I cringe whenever I see someone use them to rescue a clearly out-of-focus photo. The focus point you choose when composing has a huge effect on the result. Make sure it lands on the most important part of the frame, and you will get more powerful photos.

Modern autofocus is powerful, but no matter how advanced it is, you cannot fully rely on automation. If you use multi-point autofocus, make sure the area the camera picks is correct. Also spend time practicing single-point autofocus so your photos are always sharp where you want them.
