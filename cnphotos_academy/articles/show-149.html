32 Great Examples to Teach You Long-Exposure Photography

Published: 2023-08-07

Long-exposure photography makes everyday scenes look dreamlike, like a snapshot from a dream. Technically, it just means holding the shutter open longer. You do not need much expertise for a long exposure, but you need a little practice and patience. Here are tips and inspiring photos to help you master the art of long exposure.

Long-exposure gear

The key to a good long exposure is not expensive gear. You need a steady tripod, a remote shutter release, and a camera with bulb mode. You may also want a neutral density (ND) filter, which covers the lens to block light so the shutter stays open longer. A filter is useful for daytime exposure, but if you do not have one, plan to shoot at night or indoors in the dark.

"A New Dawn" ©Mark Coe

Plan your shot and framing before you shoot

Unlike a normal shot, a long exposure takes at least a few minutes. Even a short one, say 5 or 10 seconds, still needs a tripod, camera settings, and a filter. You may need a few more minutes to set the exposure.

For this reason, it is a good idea to plan your shot and framing before you prepare and shoot. Otherwise you may spend minutes on a long exposure, only to find another angle would have been better.

When you plan, remember that a good long exposure usually has both moving and still things. You can have moving clouds or water against a still mountain or skyscraper. The contrast between solid and blurred creates drama and shows the passing of time.

At night, light sources can also be moving elements. To the eye they look still, but the light waves really move in a direction. Your camera records that movement, so the light looks brighter and more alive in the photo. Like water or clouds, this light gives the viewer a sense of time passing.

"Milky Way Maintenance" ©Stephen Humpleby

Finding the right balance between motion and stillness takes time. You must predict how a scene will change over time, which is a challenge even for experienced photographers. If your first few shots are not right, do not give up. Each shot helps you know the place better. When you finally catch a great long exposure, the earlier work is worth it.

Types of long exposure

If the idea of long exposure excites you but you do not know where to start, first narrow down what kind you want. Long exposure falls into seven types: flowing water, astrophotography, light painting, light trails from moving vehicles, other light sources, architecture, and indoor long exposure.

1. Long exposure: flowing water

The sea, waterfalls, fountains, and rivers are classic subjects. Flowing water is popular because it is beautiful and predictable, and that makes planning easier.

But the popularity of water long exposure means the bar is higher. To stand out, you need creative content. Try interesting angles. Put the camera high, low, or in an unusual spot. You can even wade in and set the tripod in shallow water.

2. Long exposure: astrophotography

3. Long exposure: light painting

On a cloudy night, you can still make fun long exposures. All you need is a light source to paint the image. A flashlight, a sparkler, or an LED light all work well.

You can also ask someone to help you paint with light. You can try it alone, but a helper helps. At least a friend can watch your camera and gear while you paint, so nothing is stolen.

4. Long exposure: light trails from vehicles

If you are stuck in a city, shooting the light trails of moving vehicles is a good way to spend an evening. It is simple. Like star trails, the main challenge is finding a place worth shooting. Instead of a grand night sky, you look for a striking road or highway.

A wide range of spots work, from bridges to winding country roads. Still, you want something interesting in the frame. The point of interest may be in the background, like a cool building, or it may be the traffic itself. Roundabouts, big highways, and unusual intersections can all be fun.

6. Long exposure: architecture

Most long-exposure subjects are limited to night or to part of the day. A beach can be beautiful by day, but once it is dark it disappears. In the same way, a lovely night sky vanishes when the sun rises.

Buildings are an exception. They are striking by day and by night.

When you shoot a building by day, include something moving in the frame. Usually the motion comes from clouds or people. For clouds, the main challenge is to watch and wait for the right weather. A good balance between clouds and blue sky gives more contrast and drama. If you are stuck with no clouds or an overcast sky, try adding some people to the frame. This gives you more flexibility.

Whenever you shoot, make sure you do not block pedestrians or traffic. This is safer and keeps you low-key. If a good shot would block people, try shooting at night. Then you do not need clouds or people, just a little light.

7. Long exposure: indoor long exposure

Most long exposures look better with motion, but indoor shots are different. Indoors, the goal changes. You are not trying to capture the passing of time. You are usually solving the problem of poor indoor light.

If you shoot a normal shot in a dim building, you have two basic choices. For a sharp but noisy photo, use a higher ISO. For a less noisy but softer photo, use a lower ISO.

If you want a sharp, noise-free photo, you must use a long exposure. Because the light builds up over a long exposure, you can use a low ISO without blur. You need a tripod for a still subject.

This technique lets you capture the eerie mood of the stained glass in an old theater or cathedral. You cannot shoot many frames to pick from, but the ones you take are higher in quality. Since you only need one perfect frame, the extra time is worth it.
