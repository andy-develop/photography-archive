Ni Haiyun: The Qing River Is Not Far

Published: 2022-08-03

Original link: https://www.cnphotos.net/academy/show-74.html

Photos: Ni Haiyun / Text: Jiang Wei

The expressive power of Ni Haiyun's photos shows itself around the work itself. It holds to an ethic of 'fitting speech.' There is no tiresome 'sees-all' or 'says-all.' There is only the faint sound of time chewing time, like a rumination. These photos wait for the right viewer.

Winogrand once said: 'I photograph to see what things look like photographed.' I often think the reverse: what kind of people and things actually bring a photo into being.

The maker of these photos carries no color of personal special experience. In the end there is almost no body. It is so transparent that it is nearly just a pair of eyes. It is no longer in any specific time. This is only a way of seeing the world, so a mountain can be any mountain, and a river can be the Qing River, or any river. A world like this can be, or can turn into, the world before anyone's eyes, or a kind of lasting world.

A line a painter draws is not limited to the line itself. It stays in dialogue with the spiritual world. When we find that mountain peaks have this or that shape, they surely affect the human spirit, and they surely have a side that connects to it. When you understand this well, you see that Ni Haiyun's gaze on the 'Qing River' is far more than a place on a map. It is a wider inquiry.

Before the lamp, a dream of the south, and I wake up melancholic as the mountain moon hangs low. Heart-stopping calm, with no show of feeling.

The images slowly reveal a real, livable world, where a person can plant their feet and live with it day after day. We must know that this world did not appear from nothing. It is the living scene of these people in the photos, long before and far longer than the history of photography. A person has to 'rediscover' it.This world, which exists as a matter of course, has long been ignored and kept out of historical view. Photography has to look at it with patience and rename all things. Moreover, this is a world where fragments of all sizes keep arriving. It cannot be reduced to a single shape. A photographer who does not untie the limits of the mind cannot enter this world.

We live in a time that moves faster and faster. The world before our eyes often feels strange, and it feels fragile. Fragility is likely more destructive than strangeness. A big tree, a river, a fine house may be gone the next time you come. So people dare not lay their feelings on them. Ni Haiyun is a man who believes easily, not a man who doubts. The facts are just as he shot them. It is like staring at a rock. If you look long enough, you slowly see subtle changes. This is what only a believer does. The gaze of a doubter is not fine. A doubter walks away early, and it is hard to outlast the changes that slowly appear after the first glance. The path of human knowledge is, in the main, a process of approaching the truth. But perhaps not all of it is, and not always. Some long roads are abandoned, and some things are lost again. To return, in a doubting gaze, to a plain rock is not the limit of human knowing. It is, however, the end of reality.

It no longer seeks an immediate sensory impression with no depth of time. It seeks the trustworthy link between a person and the world they live in. In this way, the small differences, like fine branches, get a chance to be caught by the lens. If there are enough of them, the viewer can sense the outside forces that keep acting on people. Photos like this can show the shape of a specific person and the specific world they live in. They enter the state of a person's being, or, put simply, they photograph the unknowable nature of human fate.

A good photographer always bets on their own belief.

I have always praised the poetic quality of Ni Haiyun's photos. What does that mean? Three poets put it plainly. Octavio Paz wrote that the modern poet 'draws his vision from within himself.' Frank Bidart felt that we fill in forms that already exist, and as we fill them, we change them and change ourselves. T.S. Eliot thought the most important thing for a poet is to see beyond the ugly and the beautiful, to see boredom, fear, and the sublime.

For a long time, language was treated only as a sign, a tool, or a medium of mass communication for expressing ideas. But true photography is always the product of movement inside language, not outside it. Thought is born right along with the production of linguistic expression.These photos of Ni Haiyun happen to hold just these qualities. They are restrained, reserved, and they rest on a vital silence that cuts through the extra noise of reality. To render the landscape, the gaze, and the posture of people as things of language is enough.

Ni Haiyun in his own words

The Qing River has long slipped from the view of people today, and it has hidden into the dim background of history.

The Qing River is a branch in Yueqing Bay. Wandering on the river, you lose yourself in its twists. I shot its mouth, its source, and the people who live in the nearby market towns. The temper of the times and some rising group feeling run along both banks, by turns real or unreal, like poetry. The Qing River is not just a beauty. It is a landscape that is not far away.
