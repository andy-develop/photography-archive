Spring silkworms wriggle and slowly spin silk. One thread, one strand, one crossing, one frame, they weave a solid painting of dense and open spacing. Ouyang Shizhong watches the silkworms with tenderness, like his own children. He witnesses them break the shell, grow, spin cocoons, become moths, and breed, and he stays with them through the whole journey of life. Ouyang is also like a silkworm under his lens. He chews on the green mulberry leaves of reality, then digests, understands, and expresses them, and reconstructs reality through images. Reconstruction means it is based on reality. It is never pure fiction. By rearranging things, it breaks the outer materiality of the object, and so reaches more truly into the inside of things.

Portraits of People

Riding a motorcycle, to the growl of the engine and the sharp smell of gasoline, Ouyang weaves through the narrow alleys between the self-built towers of the Pearl River Delta. With the eyes of an outsider, he looks at these higgledy-piggledy buildings of mixed styles. Riding through tight gaps and messy layouts, Ouyang moves like blood in the fine vessels of the town. He touches the pulse of how this frontier of reform and opening up gradually grew rich.

© Ouyang Shizhong. Luxury Houses series, 23.

It is true that this is a precious architectural archive of the Lingnan water town. But its effect on the viewer goes far beyond the value of a document. With passport-style shots of the buildings, he completes the imagination and portrayal of the absent owners. The number of floors and the quality of materials, and the brand of car parked in the yard, reveal the owner's wealth. The style of design, the calligraphy and paintings on the wall, and the plants around it are like an entrance into the owner's inner world.

© Ouyang Shizhong. Luxury Houses series: Guicheng Diejiao Village.

New Chinese, European, Japanese, and mixed styles show not only the creativity and varied taste that amaze even architects, but also the complex ideas born from the exchange and clash of local and foreign culture. Each building is so different. It shows the spiritual need of individuals to seek their own personality once they have become materially rich. Yet the paradox is clear. The more obvious the deliberate differences, the harder it is to escape the shared likeness underneath.

© Ouyang Shizhong. Luxury Houses series: Taiping Village New West District.

If the Luxury Houses series shows portraits of individuals, then the Turning Sea into Land and Number One series are group pictures. The dust sheets on the land roll like waves in the wind. What surges in is not a gift of nature. It is the endless human desire to open up new territory. The dense pipes lie like earthworms baked in the scorching sun, lifeless. They also look like needles, straight into the viewer, causing discomfort and even pain.

© Ouyang Shizhong. New Territory: Turning Sea into Land series, 08.

© Ouyang Shizhong. New Territory: Turning Sea into Land series, 20.

© Ouyang Shizhong. New Territory: Turning Sea into Land series, 23.

Both conquer the land. Turning Sea into Land stages a modern version of Jingwei filling the sea. Number One opens a deeper social metaphor. Number One is the start of a road. Its uniqueness and its role as the front entrance give the buildings there an unusual social standing. An electric power company, still under construction, splits a mountain built over hundreds of millions of years in a short time. Ouyang sharply catches this strong social force that carries the silent cry of 'man conquers nature'. Through gloomy weather, a silent setting, and equipment still bound and unopened, he builds a cold, oppressive mood. Number One thus rises above the building itself. It points to the power and capital behind it. It is a more secret inner portrait of people.

Looking at Time and Space

In the woods, the branches tangle and twist, holding still. It seems that through the bird song you can faintly hear these reaching branches growing inch by inch, slow and strong. Here time itself seems to slow down. It slows so much that you wonder if this deep forest looked the same a thousand years ago. You cannot help but feel that one day in the mountains is a thousand years in the world.

© Ouyang Shizhong. Mountain Water series: Spirit.

© Ouyang Shizhong. Mountain Water series: Mystery.

Through the medium of trees, Ouyang links on one side to mountains, and seeks in caves the echo of ancient times. On the other side he links to water, and follows the stream upstream through Ma Yuan's Water Album. Cold Pond Clear and Shallow, Fine Wind on Dongting, Vast Misty Waves. The water in Ma Yuan's brush is lively and takes a hundred forms. It trickles, or rises in wind-driven waves, carrying the breath of Song culture toward you.

Southern Song. Ma Yuan, Water Album.

© Ouyang Shizhong. Mountain Water series: Twelve Water Scrolls.

© Ouyang Shizhong. Mountain Water series: Twelve Sea Views.

Today the exact places where Ma Yuan framed his views have faded into the long river of history. But Ouyang gathers the different forms of the water on the Fuchun River, and combines them in post-production into a modern Twelve Water Scrolls. In a trance, you hear the water ripple. The back of Ma Yuan standing by the water appears and fades. This pool in front of you seems to flow straight out of a thousand years ago.

© Ouyang Shizhong. Kiln Series: Dragon Kiln series, 1.

© Ouyang Shizhong. Kiln Series: Dragon Kiln series, 3.

© Ouyang Shizhong. Kiln Series: Dragon Kiln series, 4.

This watching and imagining of time and space shows even more clearly in the Kiln Series, and reaches further back. The deep dragon kilns look like wormholes of time and space. Gazing into their depth, you do not know where they lead. The unknown gives Ouyang inspiration. He turns the dragon kilns into tunnels that cross time and space, and uses the relics and specimens he shoots as keys to open time and space, inviting the viewer to travel with him. Longquan porcelain takes us back to the Song. A seismograph leads us further back to the Eastern Han. Relics from different periods turn the static gaze from the kiln cave into a dynamic journey through time. Even more cleverly, some specimens face the deep cave, and some face the entrance. This turns a one-way crossing into a two-way crisscross. Perhaps in the next second, a civilization from another era brushes past you. Besides horizontal time, deep-sea fish, land dinosaurs, and sky birds widen the dimensions and open up vertical space. The view of time and space in the Kiln Series matches Wang Yangming's saying that my mind is the universe.

© Ouyang Shizhong. Lurking series, 25.

A quiet nature gives Ouyang a precise kind of delicacy. He senses the emotion carried by objects with sensitivity, and passes it to the viewer through internalization and translation. In Lurking, he sets hard industrial equipment against soft textiles, above and below. Objects that have nothing to do with one another produce a subtle chemical reaction because of echoes of color, shape, or direction.

© Ouyang Shizhong. Lurking series, 21.

© Ouyang Shizhong. Lurking series, 2.

Such simple and direct juxtaposition opens up new possibilities, and opens a window into personal experience. The combination of concrete with concrete instead sparks abstract imagination. It maps the inner world of each different person. This is exactly the wonderful experience that Ouyang's translation of reality through imagery brings.

About the Photographer

Ouyang Shizhong, born in 1972. He is a member of the Chinese Photographers Association, the Chinese Calligraphers Association, and the Zhejiang Photographers Association. He won the Best Work Golden Wings Award at the 9th Dali International Photography Exhibition and the Excellent Work Award at the 21st Pingyao International Photography Festival. His work was selected for the TOP20 New Talent in Chinese Contemporary Photography exhibition and other shows.
