Where Did the Spring Festival Feeling Go? Old Photos of Chinese New Year from the 1950s to 1980s, Simple and Precious

Published: 2022-02-06

However Chinese New Year changes,

it holds a deep feeling in Chinese people that never fades,

a rich flavor brewed over the years.

Today I share some old photos of past Spring Festivals,

to savor the simple, happy taste of those new years,

and recall the warm memories of new years in a simpler time.

The 1950s,

more than 60 years ago now.

Back then,

goods were not as plentiful as today,

and this is how people celebrated the Spring Festival:

▼ In 1950, Beijing welcomed the first Spring Festival of the new China. That year, Chairman Mao New Year prints were very popular.

▼ People with a bit of schooling would write couplets for everyone. They were not calligraphers, but every stroke was done with care.

▼ On New Year Eve, the family gathered, stayed up around the stove, and warmth filled the air. This is a family in Tianjin at their reunion dinner in the 1953 Spring Festival.

▼ Four generations sat together as one big family for the Spring Festival.

▼ For the new year, besides good food, adults made toys for the children. Families with a little spare money just bought them. Simple broadswords, masks, small pinwheels, and not-so-fancy dolls were enough to keep a group of children playing with joy.

▼ Wearing new clothes for the festival, from Red Flag Market to the later department stores, was the height of fashion. Ordinary families bought new clothes only for the new year, so having new clothes to wear was a great joy.

▼ Of course, new clothes were not only bought in stores. A mother skilled hands could make one. For a child, the best part of the new year was wearing new clothes, and many mothers spent countless nights to make it happen.

▼ Trying on the new clothes mother just made, how happy that moment was.

▼ Setting off firecrackers seemed to be the thing boys looked forward to most as children.

▼ Gathering up unused firecrackers was the boys adventure.

▼ Three children in new clothes set off firecrackers, and two little girls covered their ears in fear.

▼ At the 1956 Spring Festival, villagers sat together and heard distant greetings on the radio for the first time. China first black and white TV set appeared in 1958, so before that the radio was one of people favorite pastimes.

▼ Back then there was no TV, no computer, no CCTV Spring Festival Gala, and no online stream. To see a Spring Festival show, people had to go in person. City, suburb, and county cultural palaces held their own gala every year. When the time came, crowds poured in, and it was lively.

▼ At the 1956 Spring Festival, Hou Baolin and Guo Qiru performed xiangsheng, the comic dialog, at the gala.

▼ Without a hall, the sky was the roof and stones were the seats. No setting could stop the joy of Chinese people welcoming the Spring Festival.

▼ For the new year, people put new charms in place of old ones. Banners reading Learn from advanced experience showed the spirit of the age. Back then, the slogan was stay at work if you can rather than go home, and cadres visited worker and peasant homes to give new year greetings. Here an old party secretary helps paste up couplets while calling on a farm family.

▼ Paper cuttings on the window were a must. Back then a skillful member of the family cut them, and children loved them. On windows fogged white, bright red cuttings made the new year mood feel so strong.

▼ For the new year, of course, you made dumplings. The elders folded them while children ran and jumped around, and the curious ones tried to pinch a few odd, misshapen dumplings. The whole family folded dumplings together, looking forward to the smell once they hit the pot. This may be the most new-year scene of all.

▼ Besides dumplings, people in the north made buns and steamed bread. Once steamed, they stamped a red flower or red character in the center, and it looked festive.

After the 1950s,

let us look at the 1960s,

an age of the collective.

▼ Work units handed out movie tickets and held parties, neighborhoods organized cleaning and buying new year goods, and put up decorations. Many people shopped for the new year together, and the streets were crowded with smiling faces.

▼ On January 30, 1960, the annual Beijing Spring Festival round-the-city road race started at Tiananmen Square.

▼ At the 1961 Spring Festival, children at the Changdian Temple Fair in Beijing were most drawn to long strings of candied hawthorn.

▼ In the past, the new year brought meat and all kinds of crisp sweets and snacks you could not get the rest of the year. Many foods were only sold at the new year. Today you can eat whatever you want whenever you want, but with so much around, the tongue has grown picky and even the flavors seem bland.

▼ In 1967, the State Council set a no-holiday Spring Festival policy. During the festival, communes and factories held all kinds of events and study classes, for a revolutionary new year.

Then came the 1970s,

when cultural life and goods for people

gradually grew richer.

The activities of the new year

became more and more varied.

▼ For the new year, the family put on their best clothes, combed their hair neat, and gathered at a photo studio for a group portrait. Those new year family pictures always felt festive, and childhood memory was carefree and free.

▼ Children set off firecrackers in the lane. By then there were more kinds of firecrackers, and the children enjoyed themselves more.

▼ The reunion dinner changed from mostly vegetables to dishes with fish and meat. There were not as many dishes as today, and they were not fancy, but every bite tasted of the new year.

In the 1980s,

the early spring breeze of reform and opening spread across the country.

People lives changed completely,

and this decade saw rapid economic growth in China.

As always,

the ways to celebrate the Spring Festival grew richer.

If there was one biggest change to the new year in the 1980s,

it was watching the Spring Festival Gala.

▼ In 1983, China Central Television held the first Spring Festival Gala. From that year on, watching the Gala became a must for Chinese New Year. So many unforgettable new year memories are tied to the Gala.

▼ The hosts of the first Gala were Wang Jingyu, Liu Xiaoqing, Jiang Kun, and Ma Ji. Back then Liu Xiaoqing was still a young woman, Jiang Kun was in his prime, and Ma Dong was still a child. His father Ma Ji was the comic actor the whole country loved.

▼ Zhao Zhongxiang gave the opening address for the Gala. Back then, hearing his voice, no one yet thought of Animal World.

▼ Li Guyi, then in her 30s, sang Homesick Love across the whole country. Even today, when the familiar melody comes on TV, people still sing along: Your voice, your song...

▼ In 1984, Zhang Mingmin sang My Chinese Heart at the Gala and brought Hong Kong and Taiwan pop music to the mainland. Overnight, the whole country knew the Hong Kong singer Zhang Mingmin.

▼ In 1987, a tall, handsome young man became famous across China overnight thanks to the Gala. With a lively song and dance, he sang A Fire in Winter, still talked about today. He is Fei Xiang.

▼ In the 1980s, the four big items, color TV, refrigerator, washing machine, and tape recorder, entered ordinary homes. At festival time, people went out to buy these big goods.

▼ Stores sold more and more kinds of goods. This is Wangfujing in Beijing at the mid-1980s Spring Festival, as people scramble to buy sweets they cannot get the rest of the year.

▼ Back then, the new year already showed such a bustling, lively scene.

▼ Back then, watching lanterns on the fifteenth day of the first lunar month was a beloved pastime.

▼ Back then, loud, ear-splitting firecrackers were sold in the city for the new year.

▼ Back then, the hottest seller during the Spring Festival was the wall calendar.

Year after year,

as the Spring Festival draws near,

everyone is busy preparing new year goods.

This set of old photos,

like a rare gift,

is well worth revisiting with your parents and elders.
