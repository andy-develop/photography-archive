Others Shoot Bad Photos in the Rain. You Can Use This Trick to Become a Master.

Published: 2025-03-07

Rainy weather usually keeps most people indoors, but for a photographer it is a great chance to take beautiful photos.

Even if you hate the rain, going out to shoot on a rainy day can be uplifting. Rain can change a familiar scene and give you beautiful subjects you have never shot before. On an overcast day, you do not have to worry about harsh sunlight. The clouds act like a softbox and give you nice, even light.

Simple tips for shooting in the rain

Keep your camera dry in the rain

Before you go out, make sure your camera is protected from water. You can buy a nice rain cover for your camera, or if you are on a tight budget, you can use a plastic bag with holes. As long as the bag is waterproof and keeps the inside dry, it will work. In any case, you need to dry your gear thoroughly after the shoot, because it is hard to get your camera fully dry in the rain.

Another choice is to go out after the rain stops. With this plan you will not shoot any falling rain, but you can still capture the mood of a rainy day.

Settings for rainy-day photography

If you shoot in the rain, one thing to consider is this: do you want to see clear raindrops, or do you want the rain to blur into a semi-transparent curtain?

For both choices, you need a fairly heavy rain. These are the best settings for rainy-day photography.

a. See clear raindrops

To see raindrops, you need a fairly high shutter speed (at least 1/250, and that may still not be enough). At this shutter speed, because your main light is blocked by clouds, you need to set the aperture to a deeper depth of field. Otherwise only your subject will be in focus, and you will not catch the rain around it.

Camera settings:

Shutter speed: 1/250 or higher (ideally 1/500 or higher)

Aperture: f/4.0 or higher, if you want to catch the detail of the drops

ISO: as low as possible, but still enough to get the exposure you want with the recommended shutter speed and aperture above

▲Golden Rain©Otacílio Rodrigues

▲Sudden Downpour©Lucia Ramos

b. Get a curtain of rain

If you want a curtain of rain, that is easy too. All you need to do is drop the shutter speed to around 1/30 or 1/15. (At these lower shutter speeds, you may need a tripod, unless you are steady enough to avoid camera shake.) Then open the aperture to a fairly wide setting, for a very shallow depth of field.

Camera settings:

Shutter speed: 1/30 or lower (ideally 1/15 or lower)

Aperture: varies with the look you want

ISO: as low as possible. If you have a tripod, ISO 100.

▲Cycling in the Rain©Otacílio Rodrigues

▲The Wall©James Drury

Either way, controlling the depth of field is key to using falling rain creatively. Adjusting the shutter speed and aperture to control depth of field well takes constant practice.

When you finish shooting in the rain, be sure to wipe the camera, dry the lens with a lens cloth, and take the lens off the camera while it dries.

Shoot after the rain

The beauty of shooting after the rain stops is that most objects are still wet and glossy, reflecting the light. Something as simple as a wet sidewalk can have a glowing sheen that lifts an ordinary photo into a beautiful one.

The wet sheen also adds apparent depth to the image, because water reflects light in many directions and at many strengths. With that in mind, when you shoot, see if you can let the light reflect off a wet surface and straight into your lens.

Photography inspiration

If you are afraid to go out in the rain, one way to find the drive is to draw inspiration from other photographers who shoot beautiful photos in a downpour. Here are some sources of inspiration for your rainy-day shooting.

Make a list of places to visit on a rainy day

This list can include places that look great in the rain, and places you want to see in different weather. For example, if you usually go to the beach on a sunny day, you can try going to the beach on a rainy day and see what the beach looks like in the rain.

If you do not like to go out in the rain, having this list is especially important. Because you like to stay home, you need a clear goal to push you out the door. With a list of places made ahead, you do not have to think up the goal when it rains and your drive is gone.

Try different shutter speeds

A small difference in shutter speed has a big effect on a fast-moving subject like rain. To catch single falling raindrops, you need a fairly fast shutter speed, at least 1/250. Depending on the scene and subject, you may also need to adjust the aperture at this speed. Try different settings and see how they change the look of the photo.

Use backlight

A gray sky can create soft light, but it also limits how strong your light is. Especially in the rain or after the sun has set, you may need to find other light sources for a good exposure.

A pop-up flash is usually a bad idea, because the rain reflects the flash back at you. Backlight is a better solution. It creates a nice glow that lights up the rain and reflects in the puddles.

Shoot vivid color

It is easy to let a rainy day look gloomy, because there is no blue sky or golden sun to brighten the photo. But gloomy is not your only choice. You just shoot something colorful, such as a bright umbrella or flowers, to make a pleasing photo. This burst of color also makes your image stand out, because the viewer is naturally drawn to the colorful subject.

Capture people's reaction to the rain

Another way to make a rainy-day photo pleasing is to shoot people playing in the rain. If you cannot imagine anyone enjoying the rain, think of children splashing in puddles, or their smile when they get wet. Their joy can turn a bleak rain photo into a happy one.

You can also catch the humor on people's faces when they are caught in the rain. Even if they look helpless or unhappy, the situation in the photo can be funny.

Look for wildlife

Like people, wildlife behaves differently when it rains. Birds may huddle in trees, and insects like ants may look for dry places (such as your home) to survive the storm. Even if they show no feeling, you can still make an expressive photo by capturing their behavior.

Bring props

You do not have to go far from home or stay outside long to shoot a rain scene. For a simple photo, try using a prop you have chosen ahead. You can shoot rain-related objects such as umbrellas and rain boots, or pick fun props such as toys, paper boats, and a crystal ball.

Shoot close-ups

Like using props, shooting macro lets you stay at home, and you can find endless inspiration in everyday subjects. When you shoot a tree branch or a spider web up close, they can be great subjects. Even a single raindrop, caught mid-splash, is worth a trip outside.

Do not be discouraged by thick fog

When rain falls from warm air into cold air, it evaporates and turns into fog. If this happens, do not give up shooting. Thick fog is hard to shoot, but it can also give you incredible, moody images.

Besides precipitation fog, after a cold night you may also meet fog, or showery fog, in the morning. This fog is usually lighter and easier for a beginner to shoot.

Find a good reflection

Rain forms puddles, which are perfect for practicing reflection photography. You can shoot great photos of buildings, people, or the sky in a puddle, especially after the rain stops.

But puddles are not the only source of a good reflection. Any wet surface, such as a rainy sidewalk or street, reflects light and shadow even while it rains. These reflections are strongest when it is dark outside, so if you can go out at night as well as during the day, you may find great shots that only appear after sunset.

Right after the rain, there are many chances to shoot dramatic images with beautiful clouds. Watch for these scenes and make the most of them. This is especially true when it combines with a beautiful sunset.
